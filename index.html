    Guides

    Prepare with a clear structure

    1

    Understand the topic

    Read the background guide fully, then define key terms and the core disagreement.

    2

    Study your country

    Note its government, economy, alliances and interests as they relate to the agenda.

    3

    Trace past UN action

    Find relevant resolutions, treaties and votes, and how your country voted.

    4

    Map the blocs

    List likely allies, opponents and swing states, and what each wants.

    5

    Record your sources

    Keep a note of every document and statistic so you can cite it in committee.

    1

    Topic background (about 100 words)

    Show you understand the issue and why it matters internationally.

    2

    Past actions by your country (about 150 words)

    Cite treaties, votes and statements that show a consistent position.

    3

    Your country’s policy (about 150 words)

    State what your country wants and the reasoning behind it.

    4

    Proposed solutions (about 150 words)

    Offer specific, realistic actions the committee could adopt.

    Write in your country’s voice, stay specific and avoid claims you cannot source.
    1

    Hook

    Open with one striking fact or question that frames your position.

    2

    Position

    Say clearly what your country believes and why, in one or two sentences.

    3

    Proposal

    Name one concrete solution and invite others to collaborate.

    4

    Close

    End with a call to action. Always finish inside your time.

    1

    Heading and sponsors

    Committee, topic, sponsors and signatories.

    2

    Preambulatory clauses

    Set context using phrases such as “Recalling”, “Noting” and “Deeply concerned”. End each with a comma.

    3

    Operative clauses

    Start with an action verb such as “Urges” or “Calls upon”. Number each clause and end it with a semicolon.

    4

    Build consensus

    Combine ideas across blocs so your draft attracts signatories and votes.

    Voting thresholds at a glance

    DecisionUsually needsNote
    Set agenda, open or close speakers’ listSimple majorityMore than half of members present and voting
    Moderated or unmoderated caucusSimple majorityChair may rule a motion out of order or take the most disruptive first
    Extend a caucusSimple majorityOften allowed once, up to the original length
    Close debateTwo-thirds majorityOften after two speakers against the motion
    Reconsider or tableTwo-thirds majorityCheck your rules for who may speak
    Amendments and draft resolutionsSimple majorityFriendly amendments need no vote
    Security Council substantive vote9 of 15 and no P5 vetoUS, UK, France, Russia and China hold the veto
    Tip: Procedure is a tool. A well-timed motion can give your bloc time to negotiate or end a debate that is going against you.

    FAQ

    I’m a first-timer. Where do I start?

    Read your background guide, research your country, and write a short opening speech. Then use the timer to practise.

    How long should a position paper be?

    Most conferences ask for one page. Check your own conference’s guidelines.

    What majority do votes need?

    Procedural votes usually need a simple majority, while substantive votes depend on your committee’s rules. Use the calculator for both.

    How do payments work?

    Choose a service, enter your name and email, and you’ll be taken to a secure checkout page to pay by UPI, card or net banking. You’ll get a confirmation email afterwards.

    Can I get a refund?

    If a service hasn’t started, you can request a full refund by email. Once feedback is delivered, refunds are handled case by case.

    How do I stand out in committee?

    Speak with evidence, listen to others, build blocs and propose realistic solutions rather than just making speeches.
